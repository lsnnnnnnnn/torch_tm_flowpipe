# Plant reachability with Taylor models: a research review for Xiangru and Huan

This project studies validated flowpipes for two polynomial plants: Van der Pol and Brusselator. A flowpipe encloses all states during a time interval, rather than reporting only one numerical trajectory. The practical questions are whether the solver can continue over the useful horizon, whether its bounds remain informative, and whether moving selected operations to a GPU improves the time of a complete solve. The current evidence covers one original, unpartitioned initial box per plant. It does not cover a neural controller, a hybrid transition, or an entirely GPU-resident solver.

The fixed contracts use 1,000 accepted steps: Van der Pol has order 4 and binary64 step `0.01`, reaching nominal T10; Brusselator has order 6 and step `0.02`, reaching nominal T20. Each has an initial remainder radius `1e-4` and cutoff `1e-10`; the symbolic error queues have capacities 100 and 1,000. [The fixed profile](../benchmarks/review/fixed_profiles.json) checks the exact source contract, live configuration, and outward binary64 initial box. The separately scheduled adaptive Van der Pol test has `h_min=0.002`, `h_max=0.1`; its time and step count cannot be substituted into the fixed comparison.

The main result is completion **with complete all-step enclosures**, rather than completion alone. The endpoint is the set at the end of an accepted step; the tube encloses the entire accepted time segment. Both x and y are retained for each view. The current strict CPU range observer re-evaluates the saved complete CPU and native Flow* Taylor-model objects. The packet GPU route supplies its own accepted-step recorded bounds, and [the verifier](../experiments/live_gpu_packets/verify_long_horizon.py) checks state continuity. Thus the plotted physical bounds have a common observation policy where model objects are available, while the solvers' internal validation and history policies remain different. The normalized [bounds](../results/review/bounds/curves.csv.gz), [width relations](../results/review/bounds/width_ratios.csv.gz), and [source map](../results/review/provenance.json) are the authority for the following numbers. They were recalculated from saved scientific runs; the branch's new full reruns are recorded separately when available.

| Plant, original B1 | CPU research reference | Packet GPU range service | Native stock Flow* | Four-channel data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Van der Pol, fixed T10 | 1,000 accepted, complete; solve 687.521 s in saved invocation | 1,000 accepted, complete; invocation 534.782 s in saved run | 1,000 complete models; solve 1.488 s in saved invocation | Endpoint x/y and tube x/y, all 1,000 steps for each route |

The seconds in this table preserve the source timing labels: a CPU solve, GPU invocation and Flow* solve do not share a calibrated start/stop boundary. They also come from separate saved dates, single hardware observations and different internal algorithms. They establish engineering scale and recorded completion, not a controlled three-way speedup. The exact accumulated binary64 step times in [the summary](../results/review/summary.csv) are slightly above nominal 10 and 20 because the binary64 encodings of decimal `0.01` and `0.02` are not exact decimal fractions. The profile, accepted records and comparison all use the actual encodings.

## How the implemented solver reaches its accepted next state

The initial interval box becomes a Taylor-model state: a retained polynomial plus a remainder interval for terms and errors that the polynomial does not represent. A polynomial ODE candidate is built for one step through the existing Picard/Taylor-model implementation. Its proposed remainder must contain the resulting image over the step; otherwise the step is rejected. On acceptance, the solver can further tighten that remainder through its existing accepted-boundary refinement. It then substitutes the step time into the model to obtain the endpoint, normalizes coordinates, updates the symbolic queue of past errors, and starts the next step. That queue can retain linear relations among earlier error symbols instead of reducing every prior contribution to an independent interval immediately. [The method-to-code map](METHOD_AND_CODE_MAP.md) gives the specific functions and the CPU/GPU placement.

The CPU controls the ODE candidate, Picard/remainder validation, refinement, queue/history, scheduler and state commit. The optional CUDA range service evaluates range requests from that CPU solver; its packet mode reduces repeated individual transfers while still returning results to the CPU chain. A later optional device-resident composition block keeps **one finite coefficient-composition operation** on device, but the surrounding solver remains CPU-led and this block is disabled by default. The latter is a reusable scoped implementation, not a whole-engine adoption. Local strict requests and a completed trajectory do not imply one formal proof of every retained coefficient or state transition.

## Why earlier horizons stopped, and what the controlled tests say

An older Van der Pol adaptive path (historical C2) completed 233 accepted steps but stopped at approximately 6.71491467, after 37 rejected attempts. A saved controlled historical revision that retained cross-step linear error relations (C3, science revision `190e067...`) completed its separate native adaptive T10 contract in 246 accepted steps and 35 rejections. At a common **fixed** 632-step boundary, C2 endpoint widths were 0.278221 for x and 0.291864 for y; C3 widths were 0.188094 and 0.148209. That same fixed test's stock Flow* widths were 0.153076 and 0.122296. These figures support a causal engineering benefit from retaining relations under that frozen comparison. They do not turn the older C3 arithmetic into a current whole-solver proof, and the adaptive completion row must not be mixed with the fixed-width row. [The mechanism table](../results/review/mechanisms.csv) and [figure](../results/review/figures/mechanism_comparisons.svg) keep those scopes separate.

The historical Brusselator SR1000 path had a different obstruction. It accepted 357 steps and then could not validate the remaining image even with further step-size attempts, before its queue capacity was filled or reset. The accepted-remainder refinement revision (C4) completed the frozen 1,000-step T20 contract; a recorded stock Flow* run also completed it. The later “C5” package was an audit label, not an implemented new algorithm. This is evidence that continuing the already accepted remainder map was decisive in the recorded configuration. We should not attribute the earlier halt simply to queue exhaustion, a crash, or a new C5 method. These history-preserving and refinement mechanisms are adaptations of standard validated-flowpipe ideas to this codebase, rather than a claim of inventing a new mathematical reachability algorithm.

## Arithmetic findings and the limits of a numerical claim

Later exact-rational witnesses found several places where a plausible point-coefficient operation could miss a real binary64 value. For time substitution, `-1+100*t` at the binary64 encoding of `0.01` has exact value `3/144115188075855872`; an older endpoint path rounded it to zero with a zero remainder. The endpoint repair encloses time powers, products and equal-term sums and charges the difference to the retained polynomial. A strict range request's certified integer-power entry now contains an exact `x^3` value missed by the older `Interval.pow_int(3)` path for `x=0x1.7d3ecfa658d9bp+9`; that old call remains an independently scoped limitation. In a sparse normal-composition witness, the retained point result of binary64 `1.1*1.1` missed the exact product; the optional resident block encloses the coefficient operation and charges its local error once. History matrix and non-unit-scale repairs were tested in a **saved Huan repair revision**, whereas a **separate audited Xiangru snapshot** still failed controlled local strict inputs. They are versioned findings, not interchangeable verdicts on the collaborators.

The repaired endpoint path checks error ownership at the next accepted boundary and rollback after rejection. Its original-box fixed full runs still reached T10/T20; all-step widths changed only at small numerical scales against the earlier CPU objects. The identified endpoint omission therefore did not explain the earlier large Van der Pol width difference by itself. That observation is narrower than “the solver is proved sound”: retained polynomial arithmetic outside the corrected entry points remains conditional, and the strict observer only encloses the objects it is given. [The detailed numerical note](NUMERICAL_SCOPE_AND_EXTERNAL_CODE.md) states each witness, version, tested operation and unresolved scope.

## Are the resulting four bounds as tight as Flow*?

The following ratios compare the packet GPU route to the re-observed saved native Flow* models on the **same fixed B1 schedule**. A width is upper minus lower for one physical bound. Each median is over 1,000 accepted steps of one channel, not over four channels collapsed into a single rank. These are recomputed saved runs, not new benchmark repetitions.

| Plant; GPU-range / Flow* width | Endpoint x | Endpoint y | Tube x | Tube y |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Van der Pol, median | 1.197741 | 1.165368 | 1.174411 | 1.183626 |
| Van der Pol, final step | 1.126140 | 1.107765 | 1.113408 | 1.111761 |
| Brusselator, median | 0.995794 | 0.991129 | 1.005946 | 1.002041 |
| Brusselator, final step | 0.968635 | 0.962239 | 0.982901 | 0.984650 |

For Van der Pol the median saved GPU widths remain approximately 17–20% wider than Flow* in all four channels, even though the full horizon completes. Some individual early or late steps reverse that relation; the final-step gap is around 11–13%. The GPU route and the repaired CPU reference are extremely close in width on that plant; the GPU/CPU median is roundoff-close to one in every channel. The Brusselator relation changes across coordinate, endpoint/tube view and time. Its medians straddle one, and its final widths are slightly narrower, so “Flow* is uniformly tighter” would be false. Its GPU/CPU differences are generally tiny but include a maximum relative width difference on the order of `10^-6` in one narrow channel. Any ranking must include the observation and internal-algorithm qualifications above.

## Which acceleration survived complete work?

Two CPU changes were measured in saved paired **full** runs, each with complete accepted numerical objects bitwise equal to its own comparator. Preparing the remainder replay before repeated checks gave Van der Pol 711.377→693.657 s (1.0255×) and Brusselator 1991.944→1454.661 s (1.3694×). Ordered tensor range work, with prepared replay **already enabled in its denominator**, gave 696.164→575.062 s (1.2106×) and 1440.102→1121.580 s (1.2840×). Each full pairing has one baseline/candidate observation per plant. Their denominators differ, so multiplying the factors into one claimed product speedup would be unsound. [The raw pairing table](../results/review/cpu_pairings.csv) and [figure](../results/review/figures/cpu_pairings.svg) retain that distinction.

GPU acceleration has an even larger difference between a local operation and the online solver. An offline device range batch, including pack and transfer, measured median local ratios 4.334×/4.339× for the two plants. A live range-service B32×20 prefix measured online medians 1.106×/1.113×. Packet mode's incremental online B32×20 comparison measured 1.050×/1.037×, below its recorded target. The device-resident composition block itself measured 42.314×/49.573× CPU-over-device local medians, but the five paired **whole B32×20** GPU-range-versus-resident online medians were only 1.138×/1.161×. Both resident targets of 1.20× were missed. These are 32 **distinct** subboxes, each with 20 accepted steps; this workload has 640 lane-steps and is not one original-box T10/T20 trajectory. [The route table](../results/review/gpu_route.csv) and [local-versus-online figure](../results/review/figures/gpu_local_vs_online.svg) explain the measured drop from fast kernels to CPU-led orchestration.

One native Flow* invocation per plant on the matched B32×20 parameters took 0.703/8.024 s. The five-pair resident median wall times were 249.890/355.177 s, approximately 355.5×/44.3× the respective one-invocation Flow* wall times. This comparison preserves a material contract gap: stock Flow* does not expose our exact accepted-boundary refinement, history replay and commit controls, though initial boxes, order, step, cutoff, queue capacity and CPU resource were matched as far as recorded. It is an engineering scale warning, not an identical-algorithm CPU/GPU speedup. [The matched table](../results/review/matched_flowstar.csv) and [figure](../results/review/figures/matched_flowstar_scale.svg) give the exact limits. The resident block has saved small/prefix checks but no accepted original B1 fixed 1,000-step horizon.

## Relationship to Huan's proof and external implementations

Huan's clean external engine is identified by `d5f0b68fcd36ba5f582733624f074728fe9720d8`; its saved strict repair descendant is `743f620...`. The audited Xiangru snapshot is separately `1c16d4...`. Our CPU, strict request, GPU packet and resident routes are separate fixed project revisions named in [the numerical version table](NUMERICAL_SCOPE_AND_EXTERNAL_CODE.md). The first-hand [clean engine paper source](https://github.com/huanzhang12/flowstar-gpu/blob/d5f0b68fcd36ba5f582733624f074728fe9720d8/docs/paper/main.tex), `docs/paper/main.tex`, states an any-evaluation-order finite binary64 reduction bound `(2m-1)u Σ|a_i b_i| + mη` under round-to-nearest, gradual underflow, finite intermediates and `m u ≤ 1/4`. Its local reduction implementation and exact-rational schedules were mapped in the recorded audit. The same mapping did **not** demonstrate all claimed retained polynomial/Phi-product coefficient inflation or a production assertion of the no-flush-to-zero precondition; refinement proposal/commit freshness could not be fully followed. The historical exact VDP and throughput phases of that clean engine audit were therefore **not run**. This is not evidence that the engine failed VDP T10.

The saved Huan strict repair passed some matrix/scale and endpoint witnesses in scoped re-evaluation. The Xiangru audited snapshot failed controlled strict endpoint/matrix/scale local inputs. Native stock Flow* is a useful full engineering comparator with its own recorded scalar-affine numerical counterexample, not an infallible truth oracle. External paper, source, fixes and our local tests have separate authorship and licenses; no external engine code or proof PDF is silently bundled into this branch. We distinguish reproducing a standard method, repairing an implementation path, measuring an empirical mechanism, and proposing an unverified future GPU architecture. The complete-device-engine feasibility goal is deferred.

Three decisions merit discussion with Xiangru and Huan. First, which **shared observation and validation contract** would support a controlled tightness comparison beyond the present common observer on saved objects? Second, which retained-coefficient and state-commit entry points should receive a complete proof ledger before “strict solver” is used without a local qualifier? Third, which CPU/GPU boundary should be redesigned if the resident local block is fast but online B32×20 remains 1.14–1.16× over packet mode and tens to hundreds of times slower than the matched Flow* invocation? [The open-question record](DECISIONS_AND_OPEN_QUESTIONS.md) separates decisions supported by the present evidence from further experiments. The current branch can be read, plotted and rerun through [one registry and CLI](../experiments/review_suite/README.md), with old failed and superseded material retained or mapped through [the recovery index](../archive/index.csv).
